About

A daily digest of the latest papers in cryptography, crypto and AI, plus regulatory and tax documents from Japan, Mainland China, Hong Kong, Singapore, the US, the UAE, the EU and the UK. Every item keeps its original title, with an explanation and summary in English, Chinese and Japanese.

Reading this site in three minutes

Does five stars mean a paper's conclusions are reliable?
No. Stars are AI pre-screening of the title and abstract; the full text is not read and proofs or experiments are not checked. They help you decide what to read first, not what to believe.
Does the topic evidence prove the summary is right?
No. It only shows that the sentence really is in the original and that the topic relates to crypto assets. The summary, document type and dates are produced and checked separately and may still contain errors.
Does "Draft or consultation" mean it is not in force yet?
Not necessarily. The document type says what this document is, not the stage of the process or whether rules are in force; the same matter may have a separate final rule. Check the original.
Why do some items have no summary?
There are three cases, each labelled on the page: still being written; the summary in that language failed the date and amount checks and is withheld; or the original has too little text for a summary.
Does "updated" in a feed mean a new version of the original?
Not necessarily. The site may only have corrected a summary, reason or label; significant corrections are marked on the item as a Correction with the reason. Compare with the original's publication date.

How it works

Public paper metadata and regulatory documents are collected on a schedule that follows each site's access rules, always linking back to the original. Explanations and summaries are written by AI from the original text only, without speculation or opinion; paper star ratings are a separate rough AI assessment (see below). Papers are grouped by field and regulation by jurisdiction; papers stay on the site for about three months. What appears depends on the sources covered, each source's collection scope and processing progress; see Sources and coverage. For very broad sources, such as Japan's e-Gov public consultations across all ministries, all EU legislation on EUR-Lex and HKMA press releases, only crypto-related documents are listed. The "Crypto" mark on regulatory documents means AI judged that the document's topic is directly about crypto assets (including stablecoins and tokenised securities), judged by topic rather than form: a meeting devoted to crypto counts, while passing mentions are not marked, and neither are job postings or staff changes that mention crypto. Each mark comes with a sentence quoted word for word from the original, which software has checked is really there. Document types, such as draft or enforcement, are shown when they can be determined; for the US Federal Register the official type is used. When an original is long, the summary is based on its first 12,000 characters or so plus a few later passages that mention crypto assets; it is not a full review, and the item says so.

Explanations and summaries are generated by AI from the original text. Dates, rates and amounts are checked against the source, but errors are still possible, so always rely on the original. Nothing here is legal, tax or investment advice.

About the stars

Stars are a rough AI assessment based on the title and abstract: each paper is scored for novelty, strength of evidence and breadth of impact, then mapped to 1–5 stars. The cut-offs were calibrated on about 20,000 papers in October 2026 and are fixed, with a monthly review; they are not a quota. At calibration about 2% of papers got five stars and 12% got four. The site only marks four- and five-star papers as Notable, with a one-line reason; no mark does not mean a paper is weak. The full text is not read: a reason only relays what the authors say in the abstract, without judging whether proofs or experiments are rigorous or sufficient, and nothing has been verified by this site. Treat the stars as a reading aid, not a verdict on quality. "Open resources" means the abstract states that code, data or tools are released; the links have not been checked.
