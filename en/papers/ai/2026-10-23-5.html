Action Learning for Scientific Simulations

In briefLearning reusable path objectives from trajectory observations for scientific simulations

SummaryThis paper studies action learning, which recovers a stochastic action from observed system trajectories to describe how dynamical systems evolve. The authors introduce LEAP, which anchors the unknown action to a tractable reference process and learns a correction by comparing observed and reference trajectories, yielding a differentiable objective on path space. The paper reports that LEAP recovers underlying dynamics and captures cross-time dependencies in stochastic physical systems, many-body orbital dynamics, and molecular simulations, and transfers across inference objectives and physical conditions.

SummaryThis paper studies G-Retriever and related methods, which use graph-neural-network tokens to inject graph structure into LLM prompts. In briefWhen to Follow the Teacher: A Method That Weights Teacher Guidance by Its Benefit to the Student

SummaryThis paper proposes Advantage-Based On-Policy Distillation (AOPD), a training method for students learning from a teacher model. Instead of imitating the teacher at every step, AOPD weights teacher actions by their counterfactual advantage under the student, reinforcing helpful actions and downweighting unhelpful ones. The objective can be estimated from standard student rollouts and terminal rewards, without extra rollouts. Reported experiments on math, coding, and MedQA show gains over distillation and reinforcement learning baselines.

Direct Inference on Admissible Sets (Diona)

In briefDiona: A Regression Method Using Direct Inference on Admissible Sets

SummaryThis paper proposes Diona, a regression method that estimates a function's state and derivatives at a query point directly from samples, with admissible bounds, instead of long global training. It treats values and derivatives at the query and neighbouring samples as latent variables in a constrained optimisation problem and establishes finite-sample coverage and, for noiseless data, convergence under dense sampling. On a six-function benchmark of three to eight dimensions, Diona was more accurate than a Gaussian process on noiseless, denser data and its bounds contained the true value at over 95% of query points for four functions. Its time to first prediction after a data update was much shorter than the GP's and a neural network's, but this advantage holds only when few predictions are needed between updates.
